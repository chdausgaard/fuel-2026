Section 8 registers further analyses in brief. Section 10 discloses what I had seen before registering. The explainer site describes the same analysis in plain language; its term for the recentered shock is “the unusual part of the rise”. 1. Questions

Main question. Did counties where the recentered 2026 gas price shock cost households a larger share of their income shift further against the president’s party than comparable counties in the same race?

This is a test of the pocketbook idea: that voters judge the government by what prices do to their own budgets. If it is at work, counties with a larger recentered extra fuel cost should have shifted further against the president’s party (the Republican Party in 2026). The predicted direction is registered (β < 0 in the model of §4); the test is two-sided.

The comparison is within races, so anything shared by a whole race is set aside: voters’ response to the national price rise, to the news and the national mood, and to their state’s price rise as a whole. A clear effect would show that voters punish local gas-price rises. Most of the treatment’s variation comes from local prices rather than exposure, so the estimate does not by itself separate a budget effect from a reaction to visible prices; the price-only variation (§8) shows how far it depends on exposure. An estimate near zero would bound this local effect; it would not show that gas prices did not matter for the election as a whole.

State-price analysis (secondary). Do differences between states’ price rises move votes against the president’s party in their own right? State averages are the prices most often reported in the news, so voters may react to how their state’s price moved more than to their own town’s. This analysis compares states within fuel supply regions and has its own randomization inference. It is a separate question, not a weaker version of the main one.

2. Data and sample

  • Units. Counties and county-equivalents in the 50 states except Alaska, which reports votes by district. Connecticut’s nine planning regions are built from towns.
  • Races. The 2026 Senate and governor races with exactly one Democratic and one Republican nominee on the November ballot. Ranked-choice races use the first round; Georgia uses the November 3 round. The registration fixes this rule, not the list: the provisional list has 66 races in 44 states, and the final list is the rule applied to the certified ballots.
  • Weights. A county’s 2024 two-party presidential vote, divided by the number of included races in its state, so that each state’s counties carry the state’s vote once.
  • Election results. 2026: official state sources; where a state’s site cannot be read or does not yet post county results, county counts published by the Associated Press through a major news outlet, named as the source. 2024 and earlier: the MIT Election Data + Science Lab; county Senate and governor results for 2002–2014, presidential county results for 1996 and repaired presidential county results from Amlani and Algara (Harvard Dataverse, doi:10.7910/DVN/DGUMFI).
  • Prices. The local gasoline prices are daily AAA averages, as archived by public third-party scrapers and the Internet Archive. I do not redistribute them. AAA did not take part in this work or endorse it. The averages are for regular gasoline, taxes included, for counties, metropolitan areas and states. I do not collect from AAA directly. Where AAA data are missing for a state and window, the EIA weekly state series is used for both windows.
  • Other sources. American Community Survey 2020–2024 five-year estimates (commuting, vehicles, income); Federal Highway Administration fleet fuel economy; County Business Patterns 2023 (oil and gas: NAICS 211, 213111, 213112); Census Bureau core-based statistical areas (2023 delineation); USDA commuting zones (2020); EIA Petroleum Administration for Defense District (PADD) sub-districts 1A, 1B, 1C, 2, 3, 4 and 5 as the fuel supply regions.

3. Treatment: the extra fuel cost

  • Price rise. The mean price over the 21 days before Election Day (October 13 to November 2, 2026) minus the mean over the 21 days before the 2024 election (October 15 to November 4, 2024).
  • Local markets. Each county’s local market is its metropolitan or micropolitan area or, outside both, its commuting zone, within its state. A market’s price rise is computed from the average of its counties’ AAA prices. A county’s price needs at least 7 days in each price window. A market with no such county takes its state’s price rise: its recentered market shock is zero, and it is not permuted.
  • Comparison groups. Each market is compared only with nearby markets of the same kind (metropolitan, micropolitan, rural commuting zone) in its state, in groups of about four (usually three to six). The groups were formed by k-means on household-weighted market centres and fixed before registration.
  • Exposure (EcE_c). What a $1 rise in the price of gasoline costs a typical household, as a percent of its monthly income: monthly gallons per household divided by median monthly household income, times 100. Gallons are household driving divided by fleet fuel economy. Household driving is commuting (drivers and their reported travel times, as round-trip miles at a national average speed, with carpool time shared among riders) plus other driving (vehicles per household times the national average miles per vehicle), with commuting scaled to 29.4 percent of household driving nationally (2017 National Household Travel Survey).
  • Extra fuel cost. Exposure times the price rise.
  • Recentered shock. For a market, its rise minus its group’s average rise. For a state, its rise minus the unweighted average rise of the states in its supply region (the District of Columbia counts as a state in these averages, though it has no race). The treatment is the recentered extra fuel cost:
B̃c=Ec×[(gs−g‾region(s))+(um−u‾group(m))]\tilde{B}_c = E_c \times \left[(g_s - \bar{g}_{\text{region}(s)}) + (u_m - \bar{u}_{\text{group}(m)})\right]

where gsg_s is the state’s rise, umu_m is the market’s rise minus its state’s rise, and the bars are averages over the supply region and the comparison group. Because groups lie within states, um−u‾group(m)u_m - \bar{u}_{\text{group}(m)} is the market’s rise minus its group’s average rise. This subtracts each county’s expected extra fuel cost under the assumed assignment process, in which the recentered shocks are exchangeable within groups and, for states, within supply regions (closed-form recentering; Borusyak and Hull 2023).

4. Model

Δvc,r=αr+βB̃c+∑gφg(Ec⋅𝟏[region(c)=g])+δμcpt+ψB̃clag+ωOilm(c)+γSc+εc,r\begin{aligned}\Delta v_{c,r} = {} & \alpha_r + \beta \tilde{B}_c + \sum_g \varphi_g \left(E_c \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{region}(c) = g]\right) \\ & + \delta\, \mu^{pt}_c + \psi\, \tilde{B}^{lag}_c + \omega\, \text{Oil}_{m(c)} + \gamma\, S_c + \varepsilon_{c,r}\end{aligned}
  • Δvc,r\Delta v_{c,r}: the Republican two-party share in race rr minus the county’s Republican two-party share in the 2024 presidential election, in points (100×R/(R+D)100 \times R / (R + D)).
  • αr\alpha_r: race fixed effects.
  • Ec⋅𝟏[region(c)=g]E_c \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{region}(c) = g], exposure × supply-region fixed effects: removes what is left, in this year’s draw, of the correlation between the treatment and exposure.
  • μcpt\mu^{pt}_c, pass-through: exposure times the rise that the state’s price history predicts, Ec×b̂s×g‾natE_c \times \hat{b}_s \times \bar{g}_{nat}. b̂s\hat{b}_s is the slope of the state’s month-on-month price change on the national average change (the unweighted mean across states), from daily prices with taxes, late 2021 to September 2024; g‾nat\bar{g}_{nat} is this year’s national average rise.
  • B̃clag\tilde{B}^{lag}_c, the lagged treatment: exposure × the state’s recentered 2020–24 shock (its rise minus its supply region’s average). State shock only.
  • Oilm(c)\text{Oil}_{m(c)}: the share of jobs in oil and gas in the county’s local market (oil and gas employment summed over the market’s counties, divided by their total employment). Cells missing from the county file count as zero.
  • ScS_c: the county’s 2020–24 presidential vote shift (Republican two-party share).
  • Weighted least squares with the weights of §2. The prediction is β<0\beta < 0.

State-price analysis. The same model with the treatment built from state prices only, Ec×(gs−g‾region(s))E_c \times (g_s - \bar{g}_{\text{region}(s)}).

Units for reporting. Results are reported as points of vote share per extra $10 a month in fuel costs, at the median monthly household income of $6,659 (the household-weighted median of county medians), so that β=1\beta = 1 is 0.15 points per $10. On the site, estimates are shown as the shift against the president’s party.

5. Test

  • Randomization inference. Under the assignment process, every permutation of the states’ recentered shocks within a supply region is equally likely, and every permutation of the markets’ recentered shocks within a comparison group is equally likely; whole markets move, so a market’s counties stay together. The state and market shocks are permuted jointly. The statistic is the estimate divided by its state-clustered (CR1) standard error. 9,999 permutations with a fixed seed (20261019); two-sided p-value; α = 0.05. The conventional state-clustered standard error is reported alongside this.
  • Confidence intervals. 95 and 90 percent intervals by inverting the test under a constant effect. Effects outside the 90 percent interval are reported as ruled out.
  • State-price analysis. Only the state shocks are permuted.
  • Power. On simulated draws with this year’s treatment (prices through October 28) and the 2022 vote swings as noise, the test detects an effect of about 0.4 points per $10 a month with 80 percent power (5 percent level, two-sided) when every state is counted, and about 0.5 on a first look without the slow-counting states.

6. Registered placebo battery: twelve past elections

The main analysis is rerun with each of twelve past vote shifts in place of the 2026 vote shift, using this year’s recentered extra fuel cost: the six midterms 2002, 2006, 2010, 2014, 2018 and 2022 (each measured from the preceding presidential election) and the six presidential shifts 2000→04 through 2020→24. Each uses the same sign as the 2026 outcome, the same controls and the same randomization inference. The vote-shift control moves with the outcome: each uses the presidential shift into the election it is measured from (for the 2018 midterm and the 2016→20 shift, 2012→16). Each uses that election’s races (for a presidential shift, one race per state) and, as weights, the two-party votes of the presidential election it is measured from, split among a state’s races as in §2. Reported: each estimate with its interval and p-value; the sum of squared t-statistics, the number with |t| above 1.96, and the smallest p-value. No special interpretation rule is adopted: the 2026 estimate is shown next to the twelve.

7. Timing and inclusion

  • First results on Wednesday, November 4 (U.S. time), for every state in which at least 90 percent of the expected vote has been counted at the time of the data snapshot, by the state’s own estimate or the “percent of expected vote” on news sites, recorded by hand with the time. A state enters whole or not at all, because the counties that finish counting early are mostly rural and might tilt the comparison within the state.
  • One update about a week after the election, adding the states that have passed 90 percent by then, and a final update once every included state has counted 100 percent of its expected vote (expected in December). I do not wait for certification.
  • The registered test of H1 is the estimate at the final update. The first results and the update report the same analysis on the states counted by then, and are marked provisional.
  • Turnout (§8) is reported only at the final update, because incomplete counts bias turnout far more than vote shares.
  • U.S. House races are reported later, on certified county-by-district results, with a cut-off of December 15.

8. Also registered

None of these replaces the main estimate.

Specification ladder. To show what each step of the comparison does: (1) the 2026 vote shift on the extra fuel cost, with a constant only; (2) adding race fixed effects; (3) the recentered extra fuel cost with race fixed effects and exposure × supply-region fixed effects; (4) the final analysis (§4–5). Steps 1–3 are illustrations, reported with state-clustered intervals and no randomization inference.

Variations of the main analysis, each with one change:

  • Minimal controls: race fixed effects and exposure × supply-region fixed effects only.
  • Extended controls: adds the shares of adults with a bachelor’s degree, Hispanic and non-Hispanic Black residents, log population density, median age and log median household income.
  • Without the pass-through control.
  • Vehicles-only exposure: household driving is vehicles per household times the national average miles per vehicle.
  • Price only: the treatment is the market’s recentered shock, um−u‾group(m)u_m - \bar{u}_{\text{group}(m)}, without exposure. Race fixed effects absorb the state shocks, so only the market shocks identify the estimate. Controls and permutations as in the main analysis. Reported in points per 10 cents.
  • Without tax-cut states: drops states with a cut or suspension of a state gasoline tax in force in either price window. In the 2026 window these are Georgia, Indiana, North Carolina, Ohio and Utah; the list is finalized under this rule once the window closes.
  • Leaving out one state at a time, and one supply region at a time (the range is reported).
  • Senate races only; governor races only.
  • Competitive Senate races: the main analysis on the 2026 Senate races that the Cook Political Report rated Toss-up or Lean (either party) on October 30, 2026, in Iowa, Kansas, Maine, Michigan, Minnesota, New Hampshire, Ohio (special election), South Carolina and Texas. And separately on all other included Senate races. Both use Senate races only, weighted as in the Senate-only variation. The difference between the two estimates is reported with a standard error that combines their state-clustered standard errors (the two groups share no states), judged against a t-distribution with the smaller group’s number of states minus one as degrees of freedom, because state-clustered errors are too small when a group has few states. These estimates have little power: on simulated draws with this year’s prices, the smallest detectable effect is about 0.7 points per $10 a month for the nine competitive Senate races and about 0.55 for the other Senate races, and the difference has a standard error of about 0.27. The ratings are read from the table in Wikipedia’s “2026 United States Senate elections” article on November 2 (saved copy). Alaska’s and Nebraska’s Senate races were also rated so but are outside the sample (§2, §9).
  • Metro-area prices: AAA metropolitan-area averages in place of county prices grouped into markets, compared in the same kind of groups.
  • Local lag: B̃clag\tilde{B}^{lag}_c adds the market’s recentered 2020–24 rise times EcE_c, from AAA metropolitan prices (county prices are unavailable for 2020). Markets without metropolitan prices get zero on this term.

Alternative outcomes:

  • Levels specification: the 2026 Republican two-party share as the outcome, controlling for the county’s 2024 presidential Republican share × race fixed effects. A second version also controls for its 2022 Republican share × race fixed effects, from the 2022 race for the same office or else the state’s other 2022 statewide race.
  • Same-office baseline: governor races measured from the 2022 governor race, Senate races from the previous race for the seat. A race with no usable previous race keeps its 2024 presidential baseline, and a county missing from the previous race leaves that race.
  • Turnout: 100 times all votes cast in the race in 2026, divided by all presidential votes in the county in 2024. No predicted direction. A second model adds RcR_c, the county’s 2024 Republican two-party share (centered), and B̃c×Rc\tilde{B}_c \times R_c; the interaction is reported with a state-clustered interval.

Governor race against Senate race. Governors have some say over fuel prices through state gas taxes, so voters may hold the governor’s party to account in the governor race. In the states with both a governor race and a Senate race in 2026 (22 on the provisional list), the two races share the voters, the price rise and the extra fuel cost, so everything about the county and the state is common to both. The outcome, per county, is the governor race’s shift against the sitting governor’s party (open seats included) minus the Senate race’s shift against that same party. It uses the main model and randomization inference, with state fixed effects, for both the main analysis and the state-price analysis. Blame on the president’s party may reach Senate races more than governor races, which are usually less nationalized; this pushes the comparison in opposite directions in states with Republican and Democratic governors. The model therefore has a separate slope for each kind of state, and the registered estimate is the average of the two. The comparison cannot detect voters punishing the governor’s party equally in every race.

Alternative price measure. Since January: the mean over the 21-day window minus the mean over January 2026. The pass-through control uses this rise, with the slope fitted through December 2025. A county’s January mean needs at least 15 days of prices. The row runs on local prices if at least 90 percent of counties, and 75 percent in each state, have them; otherwise it runs on state prices.

How much the gas-tax cuts may have softened the swing. Five states had a cut or suspension of their gasoline tax in force throughout the 2026 price window: Georgia, Indiana, North Carolina, Ohio and Utah. For each, the relief is the cut in cents per gallon (a percentage cut is converted at the state’s average pump price in the window, net of the federal tax). Each county’s extra fuel cost would have been higher by its exposure times the relief; multiplied by the main estimate, this gives how much less the vote shifted against the president’s party because of the cut, averaged over the state’s voters, with a range from the main estimate’s 95 percent interval. It rests on three plain assumptions: the whole cut reached the pump (if stations kept part of it, the calculation overstates the softening); voters respond to a cent of tax relief as to a cent of any price change; and the main estimate, which comes from differences between nearby markets, carries over to a change shared by a whole state.

U.S. House races. The main model applied to House races on their own, with district fixed effects in place of race fixed effects; reported later (§7).

Context, not a test. To put the estimate in context, I compare it and its interval with the per-dollar effects of other money gains and losses on incumbents’ votes in published studies, collected and converted under a protocol filed with this registration.

Exploratory. Any analysis not registered here is labelled exploratory. Planned in advance: the main model on each of the nine competitive Senate races alone, shown against the estimate for the other Senate races. Each rests on a few comparison groups, so these estimates are noisy.

9. Exclusions and missing data

Alaska is excluded, and the race rule decides which races enter. A state with no price data for a window leaves the sample with a recorded reason. A county with no two-party votes in a race, or with a missing 2024 baseline share or exposure, leaves that race. No observations are excluded as outliers. Values are not imputed, with one exception: in the second levels specification, a county missing from the mapped 2022 race takes that race’s weighted mean share, so it adds no information. Markets without county prices follow the rule in §3. Oil and gas cells missing from the county file count as zero; the county file omits about an eighth of the oil and gas establishments that the state file shows, so the oil and gas share is understated in some producing areas. Georgia’s two-day tax suspension inside the 2024 price window is ignored.

10. What I had seen before registering

The 2026 election results do not exist at registration. The 2026 prices and exposure had been observed. Historical election results were analysed throughout the design phase. These include the per-cycle estimates for 2018–2024 and their pooled estimate, and the placebo battery under several candidate designs. The following choices were made after seeing historical results; a design history filed with this registration records each in date order, with what had been seen before it:

  • comparing markets within groups of nearby markets of the same kind, adopted after an earlier comparison failed its past-election placebos;
  • county prices grouped into local markets as the main local prices, with metropolitan-area prices as a variation;
  • the lagged treatment at the state level only: with a metropolitan lag added, the placebo battery failed on the design of the time (sum of squared t 27.6 against 11.1 without it; 2004→08 presidential placebo t = −3.98), probably because the treatment and the lag share the October 2024 price window, so the metropolitan lag is reported as a variation (§8);
  • the 2020–24 vote shift as a control, with the previous-midterm shift retired;
  • the outcome forms among the alternative outcomes, and since January as the one alternative price measure;
  • the state-price analysis as a separate question;
  • the minimum of 7 days of county prices in each window, set on price coverage after the placebo battery had been seen on that minimum for the 2026 window;
  • the vote-shift control on every row of the placebo battery. Until October 31 the six presidential shifts and the 2002 midterm had none. It was added so that all twelve rows follow the 2026 model, after the battery had been seen without it and before any estimate with it existed.

The oil and gas control at the market level, the turnout outcome, and the comparison of governor and Senate races on the same ballot, which replaced an outcome measured against the party holding each Senate or governor seat (October 26–27), were decided on substantive grounds, before any estimate using them existed. So were the price-only variation and the turnout interaction with the 2024 Republican share (October 30). On November 2, after questions from reporters about the draft, the first results moved from Thursday to Wednesday, the updates were fixed at one about a week after the election and a final one, and the competitive-Senate-race analysis was added; no 2026 vote existed then. On November 2, after the code was public and before filing, I also ran the main design on two past elections with county prices, as exploratory comparisons: the 2022 midterm (the 2020 to 2022 price change) and the 2024 presidential vote (2020 to 2024). The 2020 end of both changes is a single archived day, October 2, 2020 (for Michigan, the mean of two later October captures). With the vote-shift control, the estimates were 0.04 points per $10 a month against the president’s party (95 percent interval −0.21 to 0.27) for 2022 and −0.06 (−0.29 to 0.16) for 2024; without that control, 2022 gave 0.06 and 2024 gave −0.30. Nothing in the design changed after these were seen. The per-dollar comparison (§8) was also completed before filing, so its result was known when I registered: 15 treatments from 14 studies, with a median of 0.23 points per $10 a month. Its protocol was fixed before any study’s numbers were read; a few rules were clarified after numbers had been extracted but before any conversion to the common scale, and each change is dated in the protocol’s change log. It is context, not a test, and nothing in the 2026 design changed in response. A separate analysis of the gas-tax cuts, planned until October 27, was dropped before any estimate existed: only three tax-cut states have a statewide race, and their governors’ parties differ, so the analysis could not be interpreted. It was replaced by the rough calculation in §8, which estimates nothing new.

On prices for the 2026 window as far as observed on October 28 (October 13–28), the placebo battery of the final design gave a sum of squared t of 8.6 (about 12 would be expected by chance if the twelve outcomes were independent; they share trends, so this is only a rough guide), no outcome with |t| above 1.96 and a smallest p-value of 0.144 (the 2004→08 presidential shift). Without the vote-shift control on those seven rows it gave 10.2, no outcome above 1.96 and 0.105. The archive of county prices is complete only on October 21 and from October 23. On September prices standing in for the window, the battery gave a sum of squared t of 10.1, one outcome with |t| above 1.96 (the 2018 midterm) and a smallest p-value of 0.104; without that control it gave 9.8, the same outcome and 0.104. Ten alternative groupings keep about 94 percent of group-mate pairs together, and the chosen grouping is not an outlier among them. The battery was also one of the criteria used to choose the design (the reshuffling groups, the outcome form, the lagged-burden control and the county-market design), so these results show how the chosen design behaves on past elections; they are not independent evidence that it is valid.

11. Code and deviations

The analysis code is public at github.com/chdausgaard/fuel-2026-code before Election Day. It covers the models, the reports, the treatment build (state and local price rises, exposure), the analysis and window settings and the group lists. After that, only the code that reads election results and prices may change, and every change shows in the repo’s history and is disclosed. If a bug in this code is found, it is fixed and the result is reported with and without the fix. Any other departure from this plan is reported as a deviation.
